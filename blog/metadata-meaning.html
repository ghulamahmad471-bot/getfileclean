Metadata Meaning: What Does "Meta Data" Actually Mean?

Metadata Basics · 7 min read · Updated 2026-11-25

The metadata meaning is simple: metadata is *data about data*. It's information that describes something else, rather than being the thing itself. A song is data; the song's title, artist and length are metadata. A photo is data; the date it was taken and the phone that took it are metadata. Whether you write it as "metadata" or "meta data", the meaning is the same.

That short definition is accurate, but it can feel abstract. This guide breaks the word down, explains where "meta" comes from, and gives everyday examples so the idea actually sticks. If you want the practical side (what metadata hides inside your files and why it matters for privacy), our plain-English guide to file metadata covers that in detail.

Breaking down the word "metadata"

"Metadata" is two parts joined together:

  • Meta – a prefix that, in modern English, means roughly "about itself" or "one level above".
  • Data – facts or information.

Put them together and you get "information about information". The prefix tells you the data is stepping back to describe other data, instead of being the main content.

Where does "meta" come from?

"Meta" comes from the ancient Greek word *meta* (μετά), which meant "after", "beside" or "beyond". Its modern sense of "about itself" has an unusual history.

When Aristotle's works were collected, the books on what we now call philosophy of existence were placed *after* his books on physics. They became known as the books "after the Physics", which gave us the word metaphysics. Over time, people read "meta" as "beyond" or "above", and eventually as "stepping back to look at something from a higher level".

That's the sense used today in words like:

  • Meta-analysis – a study of other studies.
  • Metacognition – thinking about your own thinking.
  • Metadata – data about other data.

When someone online says a joke is "very meta", they mean the same thing: it refers to itself.

When did the word "metadata" appear?

"Metadata" is a computer-age word. It is usually credited to computer scientist Philip Bagley, who used it in a 1968 report on programming language concepts. From there it spread through computing and databases, and later into libraries, archives, photography and the web.

The idea is much older than the word, though. Library catalogue cards, the label on a museum exhibit and the spine of a book all did the job of metadata long before computers existed.

Everyday metadata examples you already use

Once you know the metadata meaning, you start to see it everywhere. A few examples from ordinary life:

  • A library catalogue card or online catalogue. Title, author, publisher, year and shelf location. The card isn't the book; it describes the book.
  • A food label. Ingredients, weight, best-before date and barcode describe the food inside the packet.
  • A phone call log. Who called, when and for how long. The conversation is the data; the log is metadata about it.
  • An email header. Sender, recipients, date and subject line all describe the message body.
  • A music playlist. Track names, artists, albums and durations describe the audio files.
  • A photo on your phone. The gallery knows the date, time and often the place because that information is stored with the image.

In each case, you can learn a lot without ever looking at the content itself. That's both why metadata is useful and why it can be a privacy concern.

The three main types of metadata

Librarians and information professionals often group metadata into three broad types, a framework popularised by the US standards body NISO:

  1. Descriptive metadata helps you find and identify something: title, author, subject, keywords.
  2. Structural metadata explains how the parts fit together: page order in a PDF, chapters in an ebook, tracks on an album.
  3. Administrative metadata helps manage the item: when and how it was created, file type, copyright and who may access it.

Some sources split these further, for example treating rights or preservation information as their own categories. For everyday files, you'll mostly meet descriptive and administrative metadata, such as an author name or a creation date.

What metadata means for your files

On a computer or phone, the metadata meaning becomes very concrete. Most files carry a hidden layer of details that the software writes automatically:

  • Photos often store camera or phone model, date and time, settings and sometimes GPS location, mainly in a format called EXIF.
  • Word, Excel and PowerPoint documents store author name, company, "last modified by" and editing time.
  • PDFs store a title, author, the program that created the file and dates.
  • Videos and audio store recording dates, device details, tags and sometimes location.

You don't see any of this when you open the file, but anyone who receives a copy can usually read it in a few clicks. If you're curious what your own files contain, our guide on how to check file metadata shows several free ways to look. For the different photo standards, see EXIF vs XMP vs IPTC.

Is metadata good or bad?

Neither. Metadata is a tool, and most of the time it helps you:

  • Your phone sorts photos by date and place because of metadata.
  • Search tools find documents by title and author.
  • Music apps show the right album art.
  • Photographers use metadata to record copyright and contact details.

The risk appears when metadata leaves your hands without you meaning it to. A sale listing photo might include the GPS coordinates of your home. A CV might show your current employer's name in the "Company" field. A document sent to a client might show who really wrote it.

That's why many people remove metadata from files before sharing them publicly or with people they don't fully trust. Metadata vs data: a quick test

If you're ever unsure whether something counts as metadata, ask one question: does this describe something else, or is it the thing itself?

  • The words in your essay → data.
  • The essay's word count, author name and last-saved date → metadata.
  • The pixels in your photo → data.
  • The photo's resolution, timestamp and location → metadata.

Be aware that the line depends on context. A list of photo locations is metadata to your camera roll, but it becomes the main data if someone analyses it to map where you go. That shift is exactly why metadata can reveal more than people expect.

FAQ

What is the simplest meaning of metadata?

Metadata means "data about data". It's information that describes a file or item, such as its title, author, date, size or location, rather than the main content itself.

Is it "metadata" or "meta data"?

Both spellings are seen and mean exactly the same thing. "Metadata" as one word is the most common form today in dictionaries, technical writing and software.

What does "meta" mean in metadata?

"Meta" comes from a Greek word meaning "after" or "beyond". In modern English it means "about itself" or "one level above", so metadata is data that sits one level above the content and describes it.

How do I remove metadata from my files?

Many apps and operating systems can remove some metadata, such as photo location. For several file types at once, a metadata cleaner that runs in your browser removes hidden fields like author, device and GPS without changing what the file looks like.
