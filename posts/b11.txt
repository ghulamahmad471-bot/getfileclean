There's no single **best metadata remover** for everyone. The right tool depends on which files you share (photos only, or PDFs and Office files too), which device you use, and whether you're happy to upload files to someone else's server. Below is an honest comparison of the main metadata remover tools, including free options already on your phone and computer.

If you want the checklist behind these comparisons (uploads, formats, quality, limits), see [what to look for in an online metadata remover](online-metadata-removers-compared.html).

## The quick answer

- **Photos on a phone, occasionally:** use your phone's built-in share options first. They're free and need nothing installed, but they only cover location and only in some apps.
- **Many file types, no installing anything:** a browser-based metadata cleaner that processes files on your device.
- **Maximum control, technical users:** ExifTool on the command line.
- **Linux or privacy-focused setups:** MAT2.
- **PDFs that must be legally clean:** Adobe Acrobat Pro's sanitize and redaction tools (paid).
- **Word, Excel and PowerPoint:** Microsoft Office's own Document Inspector, plus a cleaner for anything Office misses.

## 1. Built-in tools on your phone and computer (free)

Before installing anything, check what you already have.

**iPhone and iPad.** When you share a photo from the Photos app, the share sheet has an Options button at the top where you can switch off Location for that share. This removes GPS but leaves other details such as the date and device model. The exact layout has changed between iOS versions, so look for "Options" near the top of the share sheet. Our [iPhone photo metadata guide](remove-metadata-iphone-photos.html) covers the steps in detail.

**Android.** Options vary a lot by manufacturer. Some gallery apps offer a "remove location data" switch when sharing, and Google Photos lets you hide or edit an estimated location. Behaviour differs between Samsung, Pixel and other phones, so test with a photo before relying on it.

**Windows.** In File Explorer, right-click a file, choose Properties, open the Details tab and click "Remove Properties and Personal Information". You can create a cleaned copy or remove selected properties. It works for JPEGs and Office files but doesn't reach every field in every format. Full steps are in [how to remove EXIF data on Windows](remove-exif-data-windows.html).

**Mac.** Preview can remove location information from a photo through Tools > Show Inspector, but macOS has no one-click "remove everything" option for all file types.

**Pros:** free, no uploads. **Cons:** usually location only, one file at a time, inconsistent between devices.

A browser-based metadata cleaner runs inside a web page, but the cleaning happens on your device. Nothing is installed and, if the tool is built properly, nothing is uploaded.

**Pros:** many formats, works on any device with a modern browser, no install, batch cleaning.
**Cons:** depends on the browser; you have to trust the site's code (the offline test helps).

## 3. Upload-based online metadata removers (free with limits)

Many websites remove EXIF data by uploading your file to their server, cleaning it, and letting you download the result. That works against the point: your private file, including the GPS data you wanted to hide, now sits on a server you don't control.

**Pros:** easy, nothing to install.
**Cons:** your file is uploaded; file size and daily limits are common; many handle only JPG; quality may drop if the image is re-compressed.

If you use one, read its privacy policy for how long files are kept, and avoid it for anything confidential.

## 4. ExifTool (free, open source)

ExifTool by Phil Harvey is the reference tool for reading and writing metadata. It supports a very wide range of file formats and is updated often. One command such as `exiftool -all= photo.jpg` strips most metadata from a file.

**Pros:** extremely thorough, free, scriptable, handles whole folders, works offline.
**Cons:** command line only (graphical front-ends such as ExifToolGUI exist for Windows), easy to get wrong if you're not used to it, and it keeps an `_original` backup copy by default that you need to delete. Our [ExifTool guide](exiftool-remove-metadata.html) walks through the commands safely.

## 5. MAT2 (free, open source)

MAT2, the Metadata Anonymisation Toolkit, is aimed at privacy and is available in many Linux distributions. It cleans images, PDFs, office documents, audio, video and archives, and it can run from the command line or through a file manager extension on some desktops.

**Pros:** privacy-first design, many formats, offline.
**Cons:** mainly for Linux; not a natural fit for Windows or phone users. For PDFs it can rebuild the file, which may change how it looks or make text unselectable, so check the result.

## 6. Desktop apps such as ExifCleaner (free)

ExifCleaner is an open-source drag-and-drop app for Windows, Mac and Linux that uses ExifTool underneath. It's simple and works offline. Check its release page to see whether it's still actively updated before you rely on it, as with any small desktop app.

**Pros:** drag and drop, offline, batch.
**Cons:** another app to install and keep updated; coverage of documents is narrower than ExifTool itself.

On Android, apps such as Scrambled Exif (available on F-Droid) remove metadata from photos when you share them through the app.

## 7. Adobe Acrobat Pro (paid)

For PDFs, Acrobat Pro has a Sanitize Document option and a Remove Hidden Information option in its redaction tools. They can remove metadata, hidden layers, attachments, comments and scripts, and Acrobat's redaction is the right choice when you also need to black out visible text. Adobe moves these menus between versions, so search Acrobat's tools for "Sanitize" or "Redact". See [Acrobat's remove hidden information feature](acrobat-remove-hidden-information.html) for details.

**Pros:** thorough for PDFs, real redaction, used in legal and government work.
**Cons:** subscription cost, PDF only, more steps than a simple cleaner.

## 8. Microsoft Office Document Inspector (included with Office)

Word, Excel and PowerPoint have a built-in Document Inspector (File > Info > Check for Issues > Inspect Document) that finds comments, tracked changes, document properties, hidden rows and more. It's the best first step for Office files because it also catches content-level leaks, such as hidden sheets, that a plain metadata cleaner won't remove.

**Pros:** free if you already have Office, catches hidden content, not just properties.
**Cons:** one file at a time, Windows and Mac Office only, and you have to remember to run it.

## Comparison at a glance

- **Free, no upload:** phone share options, Windows Properties, browser-based cleaners, ExifTool, MAT2, ExifCleaner.
- **Widest format coverage:** ExifTool, then MAT2 and browser-based cleaners.
- **Easiest for non-technical users:** browser-based cleaners and built-in options.
- **Best for PDFs that need redaction:** Acrobat Pro (paid).
- **Uploads your files:** upload-based websites. Avoid them for sensitive files.

## How to choose

1. **List what you actually share.** If it's only phone photos, a built-in option or a simple photo cleaner may be enough. If it's contracts, CVs and invoices, you need document support.
2. **Decide whether uploading is acceptable.** For anything personal or confidential, choose a tool that works locally.
3. **Test the result.** Clean one file, then check it with a metadata viewer. Our guide on [how to check file metadata](how-to-check-file-metadata.html) shows several ways.
4. **Make it a habit.** The best metadata cleaner is the one you'll actually use every time.

## FAQ

### What is the best free metadata remover?

For most people, a browser-based metadata cleaner that works locally is the easiest free option because it covers photos, documents and videos without an install. Technical users who want maximum control should use ExifTool.

### Are online metadata removers safe?

It depends on whether they upload your file. Tools that process files in your browser keep them on your device. Upload-based tools send your file to a server, so avoid them for anything sensitive.

### Does removing metadata reduce quality?

A good metadata remover doesn't change image pixels or video frames. Tools that re-compress images can reduce quality, so test with one file first.

### Is one tool enough for all my files?

Often, yes, but documents are a special case. A metadata cleaner removes properties such as author and dates, while Office's Document Inspector or Acrobat's redaction also handles hidden content like comments, tracked changes and hidden text. For important documents, use both.

The **metadata meaning** is simple: metadata is *data about data*. It's information that describes something else, rather than being the thing itself. A song is data; the song's title, artist and length are metadata. A photo is data; the date it was taken and the phone that took it are metadata. Whether you write it as "metadata" or "meta data", the meaning is the same.

That short definition is accurate, but it can feel abstract. This guide breaks the word down, explains where "meta" comes from, and gives everyday examples so the idea actually sticks. If you want the practical side (what metadata hides inside your files and why it matters for privacy), our [plain-English guide to file metadata](what-is-metadata.html) covers that in detail.

## Breaking down the word "metadata"

"Metadata" is two parts joined together:

- **Meta** – a prefix that, in modern English, means roughly "about itself" or "one level above".
- **Data** – facts or information.

Put them together and you get "information about information". The prefix tells you the data is stepping back to describe other data, instead of being the main content.

## Where does "meta" come from?

"Meta" comes from the ancient Greek word *meta* (μετά), which meant "after", "beside" or "beyond". Its modern sense of "about itself" has an unusual history.

When Aristotle's works were collected, the books on what we now call philosophy of existence were placed *after* his books on physics. They became known as the books "after the Physics", which gave us the word **metaphysics**. Over time, people read "meta" as "beyond" or "above", and eventually as "stepping back to look at something from a higher level".

That's the sense used today in words like:

- **Meta-analysis** – a study of other studies.
- **Metacognition** – thinking about your own thinking.
- **Metadata** – data about other data.

When someone online says a joke is "very meta", they mean the same thing: it refers to itself.

## When did the word "metadata" appear?

"Metadata" is a computer-age word. It is usually credited to computer scientist Philip Bagley, who used it in a 1968 report on programming language concepts. From there it spread through computing and databases, and later into libraries, archives, photography and the web.

The idea is much older than the word, though. Library catalogue cards, the label on a museum exhibit and the spine of a book all did the job of metadata long before computers existed.

## Everyday metadata examples you already use

Once you know the metadata meaning, you start to see it everywhere. A few examples from ordinary life:

- **A library catalogue card or online catalogue.** Title, author, publisher, year and shelf location. The card isn't the book; it describes the book.
- **A food label.** Ingredients, weight, best-before date and barcode describe the food inside the packet.
- **A phone call log.** Who called, when and for how long. The conversation is the data; the log is metadata about it.
- **An email header.** Sender, recipients, date and subject line all describe the message body.
- **A music playlist.** Track names, artists, albums and durations describe the audio files.
- **A photo on your phone.** The gallery knows the date, time and often the place because that information is stored with the image.

In each case, you can learn a lot without ever looking at the content itself. That's both why metadata is useful and why it can be a privacy concern.

## The three main types of metadata

Librarians and information professionals often group metadata into three broad types, a framework popularised by the US standards body NISO:

1. **Descriptive metadata** helps you find and identify something: title, author, subject, keywords.
2. **Structural metadata** explains how the parts fit together: page order in a PDF, chapters in an ebook, tracks on an album.
3. **Administrative metadata** helps manage the item: when and how it was created, file type, copyright and who may access it.

Some sources split these further, for example treating rights or preservation information as their own categories. For everyday files, you'll mostly meet descriptive and administrative metadata, such as an author name or a creation date.

## What metadata means for your files

On a computer or phone, the metadata meaning becomes very concrete. Most files carry a hidden layer of details that the software writes automatically:

- **Photos** often store camera or phone model, date and time, settings and sometimes GPS location, mainly in a format called EXIF.
- **Word, Excel and PowerPoint documents** store author name, company, "last modified by" and editing time.
- **PDFs** store a title, author, the program that created the file and dates.
- **Videos and audio** store recording dates, device details, tags and sometimes location.

You don't see any of this when you open the file, but anyone who receives a copy can usually read it in a few clicks. If you're curious what your own files contain, our guide on [how to check file metadata](how-to-check-file-metadata.html) shows several free ways to look. For the different photo standards, see [EXIF vs XMP vs IPTC](exif-vs-xmp-vs-iptc.html).

## Is metadata good or bad?

Neither. Metadata is a tool, and most of the time it helps you:

- Your phone sorts photos by date and place because of metadata.
- Search tools find documents by title and author.
- Music apps show the right album art.
- Photographers use metadata to record copyright and contact details.

The risk appears when metadata leaves your hands without you meaning it to. A sale listing photo might include the GPS coordinates of your home. A CV might show your current employer's name in the "Company" field. A document sent to a client might show who really wrote it.

That's why many people remove metadata from files before sharing them publicly or with people they don't fully trust. A **metadata remover** strips those hidden fields while leaving the visible content untouched. ## Metadata vs data: a quick test

If you're ever unsure whether something counts as metadata, ask one question: **does this describe something else, or is it the thing itself?**

- The words in your essay → data.
- The essay's word count, author name and last-saved date → metadata.
- The pixels in your photo → data.
- The photo's resolution, timestamp and location → metadata.

Be aware that the line depends on context. A list of photo locations is metadata to your camera roll, but it becomes the main data if someone analyses it to map where you go. That shift is exactly why metadata can reveal more than people expect.

For quick definitions of related terms such as EXIF, XMP, ID3 and IPTC, see our [metadata glossary](metadata-glossary.html).

Metadata is the information stored inside a file that describes it, rather than the content you see. A good metadata cleaner removes it while leaving the picture, text or video untouched. Typical examples:

## What's hidden in a photo

Most photos carry a block of EXIF data written by the camera, and sometimes XMP or IPTC data added by editing apps. Depending on the device and settings, that can include:

- **GPS location:** latitude, longitude and often altitude, precise enough to point to a single house.
- **Date and time:** when the photo was taken, sometimes down to the second, and sometimes the time zone.
- **Device details:** make and model, lens, and on some cameras a serial number.
- **Camera settings:** exposure, ISO, focal length, flash.
- **Software and edit history:** the app used to edit or export the image.
- **A small preview thumbnail:** which, in some files, can still show the original before cropping.

Some of this is harmless. The location is the part that causes real problems, because a few photos taken at home are enough to reveal your address.

Tools that convert or recompress images can reduce quality slightly; we explain the difference in [does removing metadata reduce quality](does-removing-metadata-reduce-quality.html).

### Can I remove GPS from photos but keep the date?

Yes. The phone share options above remove only location, and ExifTool can delete GPS fields alone with `exiftool -gps:all= photo.jpg`. A full metadata cleaner removes everything, which is the safer choice when you don't need the date.

### Is it safe to use an online photo metadata cleaner?

It depends on where the cleaning happens. A metadata cleaner that runs in your browser keeps photos on your device. Upload-based sites send the original, including the GPS data, to their server, so avoid them for personal photos.

### Can removed photo metadata be recovered?

Not from the cleaned file. Once the EXIF block is gone it isn't hidden anywhere in that copy. Your original photo still has it, so keep or delete the original as you see fit.

A **metadata cleaner for PDF** files should do more than blank the Author box. A properly cleaned PDF has no document properties, no XMP metadata stream and no older saved versions hidden at the end of the file, while the pages look exactly the same. This guide explains what a PDF metadata cleaner needs to remove, where common methods fall short, and how to check the result yourself in a couple of minutes.

If you only want the quick steps, see [how to remove metadata from PDF files](remove-metadata-from-pdf.html). This article is about doing it thoroughly and knowing that it worked.

## Why PDFs are harder to clean than photos

A JPEG keeps most of its metadata in one or two blocks near the start of the file. A PDF is a collection of objects (pages, fonts, images, annotations, metadata) linked together, and personal information can sit in several of them at once:

- **The document information dictionary:** Title, Author, Subject, Keywords, Creator, Producer, CreationDate and ModDate. This is what most viewers show under Document Properties.
- **The XMP metadata stream:** a separate XML copy of many of the same fields, often with document and instance IDs and sometimes editing history. Read more in [PDF XMP metadata](pdf-xmp-metadata.html).
- **Page-level and object-level metadata:** XMP or private application data (often called PieceInfo) attached to individual pages, images or other objects by design and layout programs.
- **Incremental updates:** many programs save edits by appending changes to the end of the file instead of rewriting it. The old values, including old metadata, can still be inside.
- **Content that isn't metadata but still identifies you:** comments and annotations with author names, form field values, file attachments, hidden layers, and photos embedded in the pages that carry their own EXIF data.

A cleaner that handles only the first item can leave a PDF that looks clean in one viewer and still shows your name in another.

## What a good metadata cleaner for PDF should do

Use this as a checklist when choosing a tool:

1. **Remove the document information dictionary**, not just empty the visible fields.
2. **Remove the document-level XMP stream** and XMP or private app data on pages and objects.
3. **Rewrite the file as a single version**, so earlier revisions from incremental saves are gone rather than hidden.
4. **Leave the visible pages unchanged:** same text, images, fonts and layout, with no re-rendering or rasterising.
5. **Tell you what it found**, so you know which fields were in the file.
6. **Warn you about digital signatures.** Any change to a signed PDF breaks the signature. See [signed PDFs and metadata](signed-pdf-metadata.html).
7. **Not upload your document** if it's confidential. A contract or medical letter sent to a stranger's server to remove your name defeats the purpose.

No automatic metadata tool should be trusted to deal with annotations, attachments or form data without you looking. Those are part of the document's content, and removing them silently could break a file someone needs.

## Method 3: ExifTool plus qpdf (free, command line)

ExifTool can clear PDF metadata with a command such as `exiftool -all:all= file.pdf`, but there's an important catch that its own documentation spells out. ExifTool edits PDFs using an incremental update, which is fast and reversible, and its documentation states that all PDF metadata edits are reversible because the old information isn't actually deleted from the file. In other words, the original metadata can be recovered.

To make the removal permanent, ExifTool's documentation recommends rewriting the file afterwards with qpdf:

## Methods that look clean but often aren't

- **Typing over the fields in Document Properties.** This usually changes the classic fields but may leave the XMP copy, and some programs save the edit incrementally.
- **Print to PDF.** It creates a new file, but that file gets new metadata (often the title and the producer), and it can flatten forms and links or change text quality.
- **Re-saving in another app.** The new app may simply add itself as the Producer while keeping the rest.

## How to check a cleaned PDF yourself

Run at least two of these checks on the cleaned copy before you send it.

**1. Look at Document Properties in two viewers.** In Acrobat Reader, use File > Properties. On a Mac, open the file in Preview and choose Tools > Show Inspector. Chrome's built-in PDF viewer also has a Document properties option in its menu. The Author, Title, Creator and Producer fields should be empty or missing. Checking two viewers matters because some read the classic fields and others read XMP first.

**2. Search the raw file.** Open a copy of the PDF in a plain text editor and search for your name, `/Author` and `xmpmeta`. If your name appears in plain text, something was left behind. This check can miss text stored in compressed streams, so treat a clean result as a good sign, not proof.

**3. Use ExifTool to list everything.** The command `exiftool -a -G1 file.pdf` lists every metadata tag it can find, grouped by where it came from. On a well-cleaned PDF the list should be short and contain mainly technical details such as the page count and PDF version.

**4. Look at the content.** Open the comments panel and the attachments panel in your viewer, click through any form fields, and zoom into embedded photos. If a photo matters, clean it before you put it into the document.

## Clean the source file first

The easiest PDF to clean is one that never picked up your details in the first place. If the PDF comes from a Word file, remove the Word document's metadata before exporting, then run the PDF through a PDF metadata cleaner as a final step. That way the Title and Author fields aren't copied over from the start.

## FAQ

### Does a metadata cleaner for PDF change how the document looks?

It shouldn't. Removing the document properties and XMP doesn't touch the page content, so text, images and layout stay the same. If a tool re-renders pages as images or noticeably changes the file's appearance, it's doing more than metadata cleaning.

### Is removing metadata from a PDF the same as redacting it?

No. A metadata remover clears hidden properties such as the author and creation dates. Redaction removes visible content such as names or account details from the pages. For legal or confidential documents you may need both.

### Why does my PDF still show an author after cleaning?

Usually because only one copy of the metadata was removed. The classic properties and the XMP stream are separate, and some viewers read one or the other. An old incremental save can also keep earlier values. Use a cleaner that removes both and rewrites the file, then check in two viewers.

### Can I clean a signed or password-protected PDF?

Cleaning a signed PDF invalidates the signature, so clean the document before it's signed. For password-protected PDFs, remove the password first with the owner's permission, then clean the file and protect it again if needed.
