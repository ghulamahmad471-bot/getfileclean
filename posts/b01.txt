**Metadata** is data about data. Every photo, document, PDF and video you create carries a hidden layer of file metadata that describes the file: who made it, when, on what device, and sometimes exactly where. You don't see it when you open the file, but anyone who receives the file can read it in seconds.

## What does metadata look like?

Think of a file like a letter in an envelope. The letter is the content you see. The metadata is everything written on the envelope: sender, date, postmark, return address. Some common metadata examples:

- **Photos:** camera or phone model, date and time taken, lens settings, editing software and often GPS coordinates.
- **Word, Excel and PowerPoint files:** author name, company, "last modified by", total editing time and template name.
- **PDF files:** author, title, the program that created the file and creation dates.
- **Videos:** recording date, device model and, on many phones, the location where the clip was filmed.
- **Music and audio:** artist, album, encoder software, comments and cover art.

## Why does file metadata exist?

Metadata is useful. Your phone uses it to sort photos by date and place. Search tools use it to find documents. Photographers use it to record copyright. The problem is that metadata travels with the file when you share it, and most people never think to check it.

## When metadata becomes a privacy risk

A photo of something you're selling online can reveal your home address through its GPS coordinates. A CV can show the name of your current employer in the "Company" field. A report sent to a client can show that it was originally written for a competitor. None of this is visible on screen, which is exactly why it causes problems.

**EXIF data** (Exchangeable Image File Format) is the metadata your camera or phone writes into every photo it takes. It's the most common type of photo metadata, and it can reveal far more than most people expect.

## What EXIF metadata contains

A typical photo taken on a smartphone stores dozens of EXIF fields, including:

- **Device make and model**, for example the exact phone you own.
- **Date and time** the photo was taken, down to the second.
- **GPS latitude, longitude and altitude** if location services were on.
- **Camera settings** such as shutter speed, aperture, ISO and focal length.
- **Software** used to edit or export the image.
- **Orientation**, which tells viewers how to rotate the image.
- **Serial numbers** of the camera body or lens on many professional cameras.
- **A small thumbnail** of the original image.

## Why EXIF data matters for privacy

The GPS fields are the biggest concern. If you take a photo at home and post it or send it as a file, anyone who opens the image metadata can place a pin on a map at your front door. Dates and times reveal routines. Device models and serial numbers can link different photos to the same person.

## Where EXIF data survives and where it doesn't

Some large social networks strip EXIF from images when they display them publicly, but many places keep the original file intact: email attachments, cloud storage links, file-sharing apps, messaging apps when you send a photo as a document, and most websites where you upload original files. You can't rely on every platform to clean files for you.

## How to remove EXIF data

You have a few options:

1. Turn off location tagging in your camera app so new photos don't record GPS.
2. Use your operating system's built-in tools on [Windows](remove-exif-data-windows.html) or [Mac](remove-exif-data-mac.html).
3. Use an **EXIF remover** like GetFileClean to strip all EXIF, XMP and IPTC metadata in one step.

## Bottom line

EXIF metadata is useful for organising your own photo library, but it rarely needs to go along when you share an image. Strip it before you send.

=== exif-vs-xmp-vs-iptc
title: EXIF vs XMP vs IPTC: The 3 Types of Image Metadata Explained
cat: Metadata Basics
kw: exif vs xmp, iptc metadata, xmp metadata, image metadata types, photo metadata standards
desc: EXIF vs XMP vs IPTC explained. Learn the three image metadata standards, what each one stores, and how to remove all photo metadata at once.
---
When people talk about photo metadata they usually mean EXIF, but images can carry three different metadata standards: **EXIF, XMP and IPTC**. Removing only one of them can leave personal details behind.

## EXIF: written by the camera

EXIF metadata is created automatically when a photo is taken. It records technical facts: device model, exposure settings, date and time, orientation and GPS location. You rarely type EXIF data yourself; your camera fills it in.

## IPTC: written by people

IPTC metadata was designed by news organisations to describe images. It stores fields such as:

- Creator or photographer name
- Caption and headline
- Keywords
- Copyright notice
- City, state and country

Photo agencies and professional photographers fill in IPTC fields, often with editing software. If you've ever typed your name into a "copyright" box in an editor, it probably went into IPTC.

## XMP: the flexible modern format

XMP (Extensible Metadata Platform) was created by Adobe. It's an XML-based format that can hold almost anything, including copies of EXIF and IPTC values, editing history, ratings, labels and the settings used in programs like Lightroom and Photoshop. XMP can also appear in PDFs, videos and other files, not only images.

## Why the differences matter

Many basic tools only remove EXIF. A photo could have its GPS fields stripped but still carry your name in IPTC, or an editing history in XMP that lists file paths from your computer, which can include your username.

## Comparison at a glance

- **EXIF:** set by the device. Contains device, settings, dates and GPS.
- **IPTC:** set by people. Contains names, captions, keywords and copyright.
- **XMP:** set by software. Contains anything, including edit history and copies of other metadata.

Anyone who gets the original file. Reading location metadata needs no special skill: many free apps and websites display a map pin as soon as a photo is opened. Common ways the original file gets shared include:

- Emailing photos as attachments
- Sending photos as "files" or "documents" in messaging apps
- Sharing cloud storage folders or links
- Uploading to websites, forums and marketplaces that keep original files
- Giving files to someone on a USB drive

- Selling furniture or a car online with photos taken in your driveway
- Sharing pictures of your children at home or school

=== does-removing-metadata-reduce-quality
title: Does Removing Metadata Reduce Image or Video Quality?
cat: Metadata Basics
kw: remove metadata without losing quality, does removing exif reduce quality, lossless metadata removal, metadata remover quality
desc: Does removing metadata reduce quality? Learn how lossless metadata removal works for JPG, PNG, PDF and video, and when a file actually gets re-compressed.
---
A common worry is that cleaning a file will make it look worse. The short answer: **removing metadata does not reduce quality** when it's done properly. It depends on how the tool works.

## Two ways tools remove metadata

**1. Re-saving the file.** Some tools open your image and save it again, which drops metadata as a side effect. For JPG files this means re-compressing the image, which can reduce quality and change file size. Each re-save loses a little more detail.

**2. Removing the metadata blocks directly.** Better tools edit the file's structure and simply cut out the metadata sections, leaving the actual image data untouched byte for byte. This is lossless metadata removal.

## How GetFileClean handles each format

- **JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF:** metadata blocks are removed directly. Pixels are not re-compressed, so quality is identical.
- **Word, Excel, PowerPoint, OpenDocument:** only the properties files are replaced. Your text, formatting and images stay the same.
- **PDF:** the document info and XMP metadata are removed. Page content isn't changed.
- **MP4 and MOV video:** metadata boxes are wiped in place. The file size stays the same and the video and sound are untouched.
- **MP3, FLAC, WAV:** tag sections are removed. Audio data isn't re-encoded.
- **HEIC, AVIF, BMP, TIFF:** these are converted to JPG or PNG, since browsers can't edit them directly. JPG output uses high quality settings.

## What about colour?

Some tools also remove the ICC colour profile, which can make photos look slightly duller on some screens. GetFileClean keeps the colour profile because it describes colours, not you.

## What about rotation?

Phone photos often rely on an orientation tag to appear upright. If that tag is removed without care, photos can appear sideways. GetFileClean rotates the actual pixels first when needed, then removes the tag. Read more about the [EXIF orientation tag](exif-orientation-tag.html).

## Bottom line

You don't have to choose between privacy and quality. Use a tool that removes metadata without re-compressing your files.

Before you can remove metadata, it helps to know what's there. Here's **how to check metadata** for the most common file types on any device.

## Check metadata on Windows

1. Right-click the file and choose **Properties**.
2. Open the **Details** tab.
3. Scroll through fields such as Authors, Date taken, Camera model and GPS.

This works for photos, Office documents, music and many videos.

## Check metadata on Mac

- **Photos:** open the image in Preview, then choose **Tools > Show Inspector**. The info tabs show EXIF and, if present, a GPS tab with a map.
- **Any file:** select it in Finder and press **Command + I** for basic information.

## Check photo metadata on iPhone

Open a photo in the Photos app and swipe up, or tap the info button. You'll see the date, device, camera settings and a map if location was saved.

## Check photo metadata on Android

In Google Photos, open a picture and swipe up to see details including date, device and location. Menus vary slightly between phone makers.

## Check Word, Excel and PowerPoint metadata

Go to **File > Info**. The right side shows properties such as Author, Last Modified By, Created and Company. Click **Show All Properties** for more.

## Check PDF metadata

In most PDF readers, open **File > Properties** or **Document Properties**. The Description tab shows Title, Author, Creator and Producer.

=== metadata-myths
title: 8 Common Myths About Metadata (and the Truth)

Metadata is widely misunderstood. Here are eight common **metadata myths** and what's actually true.

## Myth 1: "Social media removes all metadata, so I'm safe"

Some large platforms strip metadata from images when they show them publicly. But email, cloud links, messaging apps sending files, forums and marketplaces often keep the original file. Platforms can also change how they handle uploads at any time.

## Myth 2: "Only photos have metadata"

Documents, spreadsheets, presentations, PDFs, videos and audio files all carry metadata, often including your name.

## Myth 3: "Renaming a file removes its metadata"

The file name is separate from the metadata stored inside the file. Renaming changes nothing inside.

## Myth 4: "Converting to PDF cleans everything"

Exporting to PDF usually copies the author and title into the PDF properties and adds the creating program's name. See [what PDF metadata contains](pdf-metadata-explained.html).

## Myth 5: "Removing metadata ruins quality"

Lossless tools remove metadata blocks without re-compressing your image. Read [does removing metadata reduce quality](does-removing-metadata-reduce-quality.html).

## Myth 6: "Screenshots never have metadata"

Screenshots usually carry less metadata than camera photos, but they can still include dates, device information and software details. See [screenshot metadata](screenshot-metadata.html).

## Myth 7: "Turning off location removes it from old photos"

Turning off camera location only affects new photos. Photos you've already taken still have their GPS data.

## Myth 8: "Metadata can't identify me"

Names, device serial numbers, locations and timestamps can be combined to identify a person, even when no single field does.
