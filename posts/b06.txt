Videos carry metadata just like photos, and video files are often shared as originals. Here's how to **remove metadata from MP4** files.

## What metadata is in an MP4?

MP4 files are built from sections called boxes. Metadata lives in a few of them:

- **User data and meta boxes:** title, artist, comments, software, device make and model, and often GPS location.
- **Header timestamps:** creation and modification dates for the movie and each track.
- **XMP:** added by editing software.

## Why not just re-export the video?

Re-exporting through a video editor takes time, may reduce quality and can add new metadata from the editor itself. For large files it can take ages.

## What stays

Information needed for playback, such as codec details, frame rate and resolution, stays in the file. These describe the video, not you.

## Viewers usually don't get your original file

Most large video platforms re-encode uploads for streaming. Viewers watch the platform's versions, not your original file, so the embedded metadata generally isn't passed on to them.

## But the platform receives everything

The original upload, with location, device and dates, reaches the platform. Some platforms read information such as recording date or location from uploads to help organise videos. What happens next depends on the platform's settings and privacy policy.

## Where originals do get shared

- Sending video files directly by email, cloud links or messaging apps
- Smaller sites and forums that host the original file
- Client delivery of video projects
- Platforms offering an "original file" download option

If you'd rather keep your location and devices private, clean videos before uploading. GetFileClean wipes location, device and date metadata from MP4 and MOV without re-encoding, so you upload the same quality.

MP3 files store their metadata in tags. Here's how to **remove metadata from MP3** files without touching the audio.

## What's in MP3 metadata?

MP3 files can contain up to three kinds of tags:

- **ID3v2** at the start of the file: title, artist, album, year, comments, cover art, lyrics, encoder, private data and custom tags.
- **ID3v1** in the last 128 bytes: a short, older tag format.
- **APE tags** near the end, added by some software.

## Why remove MP3 tags?

- Recordings from your phone or recorder can include your name or device.
- Cover art and comments can carry details you didn't mean to share.
- Encoder information shows what software you used.
- Private data frames from some apps can contain IDs.

## Remove MP3 metadata without re-encoding

GetFileClean cuts the ID3v2, ID3v1 and APE tags out of the file. The audio frames aren't changed, so sound quality is identical.

1. Drop MP3 files into GetFileClean.
2. See the artist, title, encoder and other tags it found.
3. Download the clean files.

## Will my music player still work?

Yes. MP3s without tags play normally, but your music library won't show a title or artist. If you only want to remove some tags, use a tag editor instead.

=== id3-tags-explained
title: ID3 Tags Explained: The Metadata Inside Your MP3 Files
cat: Video & Audio
kw: id3 tags explained, what are id3 tags, id3v2 vs id3v1, mp3 metadata fields, id3 frames
desc: ID3 tags explained: ID3v1 vs ID3v2, common frames like TIT2, TPE1 and APIC, what they reveal and how to view or remove MP3 metadata.
---
**ID3 tags** are the metadata format used by MP3 files. They let music players show a song's title, artist and cover art.

## ID3v1 vs ID3v2

- **ID3v1:** a fixed 128-byte block at the end of the file. It holds a short title, artist, album, year, comment and genre.
- **ID3v2:** a flexible block at the start of the file, made of frames. It can hold many fields, images and long text. Versions 2.3 and 2.4 are the most common.

## Common ID3v2 frames

- **TIT2:** title
- **TPE1:** artist
- **TALB:** album
- **TYER / TDRC:** year or recording date
- **TENC / TSSE:** encoded by and encoder software
- **COMM:** comments
- **APIC:** attached picture, usually cover art
- **USLT:** lyrics
- **PRIV:** private data added by software
- **TXXX:** custom text fields
- **GEOB:** embedded objects

## Privacy concerns

Voice recordings, interviews and podcast drafts can carry the recorder's name, software and dates. Private and custom frames may contain identifiers that aren't visible in most players.

## How to view and remove ID3 tags

Most music players and file properties windows show common tags. GetFileClean shows the main tags it finds and removes all ID3v1, ID3v2 and APE tags without changing the audio. See [remove metadata from MP3](remove-metadata-mp3.html).

---
High-quality audio formats carry their own kinds of metadata. Here's how to **remove metadata from FLAC and WAV** files.

## FLAC metadata

FLAC files start with metadata blocks:

- **Vorbis comments:** tags like ARTIST, TITLE, ALBUM, DATE and ENCODER, plus any custom fields.
- **Picture blocks:** embedded cover art.
- **Application blocks:** data from specific software.

The stream info and seek table blocks are technical and needed for playback.

## WAV metadata

WAV files are made of chunks. Metadata chunks include:

- **LIST/INFO:** artist, title, comments, software and dates.
- **bext (Broadcast Wave):** originator, description, origination date and time, used by professional recorders.
- **iXML:** detailed production data from field recorders, such as scene, take and notes.
- **id3:** some software adds ID3 tags to WAV files.
- **XMP:** from Adobe audio software.

## Remove audio metadata losslessly

GetFileClean removes Vorbis comments, pictures and app blocks from FLAC, and LIST, bext, iXML, ID3 and XMP chunks from WAV. The audio data isn't touched, so quality is identical.

## Professional audio note

Broadcast and film workflows sometimes rely on bext and iXML data. Clean copies you share externally, not your production masters.

Podcasters deal with two kinds of **audio metadata**: tags you want listeners to see, and hidden details you don't want to share.

## Metadata worth keeping in published episodes

- Episode title
- Show name as artist or album
- Cover art
- Episode number and year

- Encoder and software details from your editing setup
- Comments left during editing
- Names of guests' recording devices or apps
- Private frames from production tools

## Draft files are the bigger risk

The files most likely to leak private details are drafts and raw recordings shared with editors, guests and collaborators. Raw files from field recorders and apps can include device information, dates and even location.

1. Clean raw guest and interview files before sending them to editors.
2. Clean draft episodes shared for review.
3. For the final release, clean the file, then add only the tags you want with your publishing tool or tag editor.
